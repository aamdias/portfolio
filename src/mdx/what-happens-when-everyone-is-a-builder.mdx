As AI makes software easier to build, I keep coming back to a question: what happens to professional builders when almost anyone can create software?

An answer might be sitting on your kitchen counter.

We already live in a world where billions of people can make something for themselves, share it with others, and get better at it over time. We call it cooking. And the fact that almost anyone can cook hasn't made great chefs irrelevant.

I think software could follow a similar path.

## What cooking can teach us

Over centuries, advances in agriculture, food preservation, distribution, and kitchen appliances have made cooking easier and more accessible. Today, billions of people cook regularly, despite having little or no formal culinary training.

Yet we still have professional chefs, restaurants, and an enormous industry built around food. Being able to make dinner doesn't mean you want to cook every meal, run a restaurant, or spend years mastering a cuisine.

Cooking takes different forms, depending on what people want from it.

### Home cooks

Some people cook mainly to meet an everyday need: dinner after work, lunch for the kids, or meals for the week. A simple, reliable result is enough.

### Cooking enthusiasts

Others love the process. They experiment with recipes, learn techniques, and invite friends over to try what they've made. Cooking is a craft and a source of pleasure, even if they never earn a living from it.

### Professional chefs

For professionals, the responsibility extends beyond making a good meal. They have to deliver consistent quality, coordinate a team, manage costs, and keep a kitchen running under pressure. A great steak is only one part of the job.

### World-class chefs

Then there are the people who push the craft forward. Their judgment, creativity, and years of practice produce experiences that others travel for and pay a premium to enjoy. They help define what excellence looks like.

These groups coexist. The home cook doesn't have to become a professional, and the professional doesn't lose their purpose because someone else can make dinner.

## Easier to build, more reasons to build

Software may be heading toward a similar mix of creators.

AI systems can already complete demanding technical tasks, as benchmarks such as [Terminal-Bench](https://www.tbench.ai/news/announcement) demonstrate. But completing a task with a defined objective is only part of building a useful product. Someone still has to understand the problem, decide what matters, make tradeoffs, and take responsibility for the result.

As more of the implementation becomes automated, projects that once felt too expensive or time-consuming may become worth trying: a tool for a small business, a custom workflow for a team, or a tiny app that solves one person's recurring annoyance.

Many of these projects would never have justified hiring a software team. They might not even need to become businesses. They just need to be useful enough to build.

There's a parallel here with [Jevons's observation about coal](https://www.econlib.org/library/YPDBooks/Jevons/jvnCQ.html?chapter_num=9): greater efficiency can expand usage enough to increase total consumption. The possibility for software is that a lower cost of building unlocks enough new uses to increase the total amount we create.

That isn't a guarantee of more jobs, higher salaries, or an easy transition. Some tasks will lose value, and some roles will change. The question is how much new demand emerges as more people can afford to act on their ideas.

I suspect there's a lot of room for that demand to grow.

## The different kinds of software builders

If the cooking analogy holds, we could see more people building software for very different reasons:

- **Personal builders** create tools for their own needs. Their software might serve one person, solve a temporary problem, or disappear once it has done its job.
- **Enthusiasts** build because they enjoy it. They experiment, share their work, and make things for friends or communities. Some eventually turn that work into a business.
- **Professionals** build and maintain software that other people depend on. They are accountable for its reliability, security, usability, and continued operation.
- **Exceptional practitioners** advance the craft. Their work changes what other builders consider possible and sets new standards for quality.

These are different relationships with the work, rather than steps everyone has to climb. Someone can build a brilliant personal tool without wanting to run a software company, just as an excellent home cook may have no interest in opening a restaurant.

## What makes a great builder valuable?

This is where the analogy becomes most interesting to me.

Making a meal and running a great restaurant require different skills. The same is true of generating an app and building a product that people trust.

An app can work beautifully in a demo and still be confusing to use, expensive to maintain, or fragile when something unexpected happens. As creating the first version gets easier, those other qualities may become a larger part of what separates good work from everything else.

Great builders bring judgment: which problem deserves attention, what to leave out, where simplicity helps, and where a shortcut will create trouble later. They also care about what happens after the launch, when real people start depending on what they've made.

More people building software could also mean more people learning to recognize that quality. Cooking a few meals can give you a deeper appreciation for what a great chef does. Building a few apps might do something similar.

Of course, the analogy has limits. Everyone needs food, while software has to earn its place in someone's life. Cheaper creation can produce clutter as easily as value. The opportunity depends on making things people actually want to use.

Still, I find it hard to look at the gap between how many people cook and how many build software without feeling optimistic. I can imagine a future with far more casual creators, more experiments, and more opportunities for people who care deeply about the craft.

I don't know how that future will unfold. But I want to help build it.

I'm already cooking. What will you make?
